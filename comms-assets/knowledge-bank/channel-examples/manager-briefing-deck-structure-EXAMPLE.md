## Structure that has worked for manager briefings

1. **What's changing, one slide, one sentence.** Same opening line as the email — a
   manager who reads a different framing here than their team gets in the email loses
   credibility fast.
2. **Why now** — one slide, the real reason, not corporate framing.
3. **What this means for your team specifically** — the slide managers actually present
   from. Concrete, day-to-day, not strategic.
4. **Timeline** — the confirmed dates only, with unconfirmed ones marked as such rather
   than omitted.
5. **What you need to do as a manager** — distinct from what the team needs to do.
6. **What your team needs to do**, and by when.
7. **Likely questions and how to answer them** — sourced from `faqs/`, not invented per
   deck. This is the slide that most determines whether the briefing lands well; managers
   without answers to obvious questions lose the room.
8. **Where to escalate** — a name or inbox for the question a manager genuinely can't
   answer. Never "ask your manager" recursively.

## Why the FAQ slide sources from the knowledge bank, not the brief alone

A brief rarely anticipates every question a team will ask a manager face to face. The
`faqs/` folder exists to accumulate real questions across runs, so slide 7 gets stronger
over time rather than being reinvented from nothing each time.

## Common failure this structure prevents

A deck that reads like the email with slide breaks inserted. A manager presenting it gets
asked a question the deck doesn't equip them to answer, and the meeting's credibility
transfers from the manager to whoever wrote the deck.
