## The frame

A three-part structure that opens most change narratives, in order: what is changing,
why it's changing now, and what deliberately isn't changing. The third part is the one
practitioners most often skip, and it's the part that most reliably lowers anxiety — an
unstated scope boundary gets filled with the audience's worst assumption.

**What.** State the change in one plain sentence before any justification. "From
15 September, expense claims move from the paper form to the online portal." Not "we are
excited to announce an improvement to our expense process."

**Why now.** Tie the timing to something real — a system's end of support, a compliance
deadline, a cost the current process visibly carries. "Because it's time" is not a reason
an audience accepts.

**What stays the same.** Name the boundary explicitly. "Your manager still approves your
claims the same way; only the form changes." This sentence does more to reduce resistance
than most of what follows it.

## Why this frame travels well across channels

The same three-part structure compresses to a 25-word banner (what, one clause of why) and
expands to a 900-word article (all three, with evidence) without changing shape — which is
what lets Stage 5's cross-channel consistency check hold multiple channels to the same
underlying claims instead of independently drafted ones that drift.

## Common failure this frame prevents

A narrative that opens with organisational history or programme structure before saying
what's actually changing. The audience's first question is always "what does this mean
for me," and everything before that sentence is read as delay, not context.
