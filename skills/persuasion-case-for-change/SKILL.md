# Garvin & Roberto Four-Stage Persuasion — Case for Change Builder

Helps build or diagnose a case for change using David Garvin & Michael Roberto's model of change as persuasion, and produces specific recommendations for strengthening whichever stage is weakest.

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

Garvin & Roberto's core insight: successful change leaders treat change as an ongoing persuasive process, not a one-time announcement. Four stages, and a change effort is only as strong as its weakest one:

1. **Framing the Problem** — establishing, with credible evidence, that the current state is genuinely inadequate and that inaction has a real cost. Without this, people don't feel the change is necessary.
2. **Presenting a Compelling Direction** — offering a specific, credible solution, with proof it will work and a clear rationale for why this path over alternatives. Without this, people agree there's a problem but don't trust the proposed fix.
3. **Reinforcement Through Repeated Communication** — the message delivered more than once, through multiple channels and credible messengers, proactively addressing doubts rather than a single town hall/email. Without this, initial buy-in fades and rumor fills the vacuum.
4. **Enabling Fair Process** — genuine two-way engagement: people get real opportunity for voice, feedback visibly influences something, and the rationale behind decisions (even ones that don't change) is explained. Without this, people may comply but won't commit — and often quietly resist.

**Common failure pattern worth watching for**: Stages 1-2 strong, Stages 3-4 weak. This is the classic "we announced it once and expected it to stick" mistake — the case was logically sound but people felt sold-to, not engaged, and commitment never materialized. Flag this pattern explicitly if you see it.

## Process

### Step 1: Intake — checklist + open-ended, per stage

For each of the four stages, ask the checklist items (tick what's already true/in place) AND the open-ended questions (to capture actual content). Present all four stages together if the user seems ready to answer at length, or one stage at a time if they want to go step by step — use judgment based on how they framed the request.

**Stage 1 — Framing the Problem**
Checklist:
- We have quantifiable evidence the current state is inadequate (data, benchmarks, financial/operational gap)
- We can clearly articulate the cost of doing nothing
- The problem is framed in terms the audience already cares about, not just leadership's abstract terms
- We've tested this framing with a few stakeholders already and it resonated

Open-ended:
- What's the core problem, in one or two sentences?
- What evidence or data supports that this is real and urgent?
- What happens if the organization does nothing?

**Stage 2 — Presenting a Compelling Direction**
Checklist:
- We can explain why this specific solution was chosen over alternatives
- We have credible proof points (pilots, comparable cases, expert validation) that this direction works
- The solution is concrete enough that people can picture what it looks like day-to-day
- Leadership can articulate the direction consistently, regardless of who's asked

Open-ended:
- What's the proposed direction/solution?
- What alternatives were considered, and why was this one chosen?
- What proof do you have that this will work?

**Stage 3 — Reinforcement Through Repeated Communication**
Checklist:
- The message will be delivered more than once, through more than one channel
- Multiple credible messengers (not just one exec) will carry it
- There's a plan to proactively address likely objections, not just react when they surface
- Messaging is sequenced/paced over time, not a single event

Open-ended:
- Who are the key messengers, and are they aligned on the message?
- What are the most likely objections or doubts, and how do you plan to respond?
- What's the communication cadence — channels, frequency, timeline?

**Stage 4 — Enabling Fair Process**
Checklist:
- Stakeholders have real opportunity to ask questions or challenge assumptions before decisions are finalized
- Feedback collected will visibly influence something, not just be logged
- People understand the rationale behind decisions, even ones they disagree with
- There's a mechanism for ongoing dialogue, not just a one-time consultation

Open-ended:
- How are you planning to gather and respond to stakeholder input?
- What decisions are still open to influence vs. already locked in?
- How will you communicate the "why" behind decisions that don't change based on feedback?

**If the user gives a narrative description instead of working through checklist/open-ended separately** (e.g. they just describe the whole situation in one message), extract what you can for each stage from what they said, state your read explicitly stage by stage, and ask only for what's genuinely missing — don't make them re-answer things they already told you.

### Step 2: Score and diagnose

Per stage, score checklist maturity: 4/4 ticked = **Strong**, 2-3/4 = **Developing**, 0-1/4 = **Weak**. Even a single missing item on a 4-item checklist is worth surfacing explicitly (name which item is missing) rather than rounding up to Strong. Use the open-ended answers to sanity-check the checklist score — if the open-ended answers reveal something the checklist ticks don't capture (e.g. they ticked "we have evidence" but the evidence they describe is thin or anecdotal), trust the substance over the tick.

Identify the weakest stage(s) — this is where recommendations should concentrate. Check specifically for the Stages-1-2-strong/3-4-weak failure pattern and flag it if present.

**If multiple or all stages are weak, sequence the recommendations rather than presenting them in parallel.** Start with Stage 1 (Framing) — later stages assume a credible, well-evidenced problem already exists, so recommending communication tactics (Stage 3) or feedback mechanisms (Stage 4) before the problem itself is established puts the cart before the horse. Tell the user what to fix first, not everything at once.

### Step 3: Output

Concise and direct — practitioner audience:

1. **Stage-by-stage diagnosis**: one line each (e.g. "Stage 1 (Framing): Strong — clear evidence, cost of inaction articulated" / "Stage 3 (Reinforcement): Weak — single-channel, one-time messaging planned")
2. **Priority flag**: name the weakest stage(s) and, if applicable, the classic 1-2-strong/3-4-weak pattern
3. **Recommendations** — specific to what the user actually described in their open-ended answers, not generic advice. If they gave you the actual problem/evidence/messengers, use that content directly in your recommendation (e.g. suggest specific reframing language, name which additional messenger to add) rather than telling them abstractly to "communicate more."
4. **Rationale** — briefly tie each recommendation back to why that stage matters (from the Background section) so it reads as reasoned advice, not a checklist output.

### Step 4: Memo/messaging brief version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo, messaging brief, or client-ready version, reformat into a short structured document (headers: Stage-by-Stage Diagnosis / Priority Gaps / Recommended Actions / Suggested Framing Language). Keep it to roughly a half page to one page. Use the docx or pptx skill if they want an actual file.

## Notes

- Don't let a user's own confidence in their case ("we've explained this clearly") override what their open-ended answers actually show — a mismatch between self-assessment and substance is itself worth flagging back to them, gently but directly.
- Stage 4 (fair process) is the one most often skipped or treated as an afterthought in practice — pay particular attention to whether it's genuinely two-way or just a listening exercise with no visible influence on outcomes, since that distinction is what determines whether people commit or merely comply.
- This pairs with the other change diagnostic skills: DICE predicts *whether* a project will succeed given its setup; Technical/Adaptive diagnoses *what kind* of change it is; Theory E/O diagnoses *why* it's happening; this one helps you *build the case* that gets people on board. All four can be run on the same initiative for a fuller picture.
