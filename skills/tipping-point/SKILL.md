# Gladwell's Tipping Point — Where to Concentrate Change Energy

Diagnoses whether a change initiative is positioned to spread — to "tip" from a pocket of early adoption into broad, self-sustaining momentum — using Malcolm Gladwell's three tipping-point factors, and identifies the single highest-leverage place to concentrate effort next.

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

Gladwell's central claim: epidemics of behavior (ideas, products, and organizational change included) don't spread evenly — they tip because of a small number of exceptional people, a specific quality of the message, and the right conditions, not because of broad, diffuse effort. Three factors, and a change effort is often stuck because one specific factor is missing, not because "people aren't trying hard enough":

1. **The Law of the Few** — a disproportionate amount of spread is driven by a small number of people with specific, distinct roles:
   - **Connectors**: people with unusually large, diverse networks who bridge groups that otherwise wouldn't talk
   - **Mavens**: people others go to for information — trusted, well-informed, motivated to share what they know because they like helping
   - **Salesmen (Persuaders)**: people with the charisma and social skill to move others from skepticism to action, often through emotional contagion rather than argument
   A change with strong senior sponsorship but no Connectors, Mavens, or Salesmen actually engaged is relying on hierarchy to do a network's job — it usually stalls at the edge of the org chart.

2. **The Stickiness Factor** — whether the change itself, as packaged and communicated, is memorable enough to prompt action. This is not about how much communication happens, but about specific, often small, changes to framing, format, or specificity that make people actually act instead of nodding and forgetting. A message can be repeated constantly and still not be sticky if it's abstract, generic, or doesn't call for a concrete action.

3. **The Power of Context** — spread is highly sensitive to immediate environmental and situational conditions, not just individual disposition. This includes physical/organizational environment (does the workspace or workflow visibly support the new behavior, or fight it), group size (Gladwell's "Rule of 150" / Dunbar's number — cohesion and mutual accountability degrade past a certain group size, so change often needs to be organized in smaller units to take hold), and small environmental cues that signal what's normal here (the "Broken Windows" idea — visible small signals of the old way persisting undercut the new one faster than people expect).

**Where this differs from the other diagnostic skills in this project**: this one isn't asking whether the change *should* happen (DICE), what *kind* of change it is (Technical/Adaptive), or how far it's *progressed* (Six Steps/Kotter) — it's specifically about spread mechanics: given a change that's already underway with some early adopters, why isn't it tipping into the broader population, and where's the highest-leverage place to push.

## Process

### Step 1: Intake — checklist + open-ended, per factor

For each of the three factors, ask the checklist items (tick what's genuinely in place) and the open-ended questions (to capture real substance — ticks alone can overstate what's actually happening). Present all three together if the user seems ready to answer at length, or one at a time — use judgment.

**Factor 1 — Law of the Few**
Checklist:
- We can name specific individuals acting as Connectors (bridging groups/teams that don't normally interact) who are actively engaged with this change
- We can name specific individuals acting as Mavens (go-to, trusted sources of information) who are informed and motivated to share
- We can name specific individuals acting as Salesmen/Persuaders (able to move skeptics through charisma or social credibility, not just authority) who are actively advocating
- These people were deliberately identified and engaged, not just assumed to exist because they hold formal titles

Open-ended: Who specifically are the Connectors, Mavens, and Salesmen for this change (by name or role, if known)? Are they actually engaged, or is the effort relying mainly on the formal org chart and official sponsors?

**Factor 2 — Stickiness Factor**
Checklist:
- The core message/ask is specific and concrete, not abstract or generic ("do X by Y date" vs. "embrace the new way of working")
- The message has been tested or refined based on what actually got a reaction, not just broadcast once as originally drafted
- People can repeat back what they're being asked to do, in their own words, after hearing it
- The format/packaging (how it's delivered, not just what it says) has been deliberately designed for memorability, not defaulted to a standard email/deck

Open-ended: What's the actual core message or ask, in the words being used? Has it been tested with real people, and what happened? Can you point to evidence people remember and can restate it?

**Factor 3 — Power of Context**
Checklist:
- The physical or workflow environment visibly supports the new behavior (tools, defaults, physical space line up with the change, not against it)
- Change is being organized/rolled out in units small enough for real cohesion and mutual accountability (roughly Dunbar's-number territory or smaller), not as one undifferentiated mass rollout
- Visible small signals of the old way have been actively removed, not left in place alongside the new messaging (old signage, old templates, old metrics still displayed)
- The immediate situational conditions (timing, workload, competing priorities) are conducive, not fighting the change

Open-ended: What in the day-to-day environment still visibly signals the old way? How is the rollout organized — as one big group or smaller units? What situational factors (timing, competing initiatives) are working for or against this right now?

**If the user gives a narrative description instead of working through checklist/open-ended separately**, extract what you can for each factor from what they said, state your read explicitly factor by factor, and ask only for what's genuinely missing.

### Step 2: Score and find the weakest factor

Per factor, score checklist maturity: 4/4 ticked = **Strong**, 2-3/4 = **Developing**, 0-1/4 = **Weak**. Use the open-ended answers to sanity-check — if someone ticks "we have Connectors" but can't actually name who they are or describe real engagement, trust the substance over the tick.

Identify the weakest factor(s) — this is almost always where concentrating energy will have the most leverage, because Gladwell's point is that these factors are somewhat independent: strengthening a factor that's already Strong has diminishing returns, while fixing a Weak one can be what actually makes the difference between a change that spreads and one that stays confined to the pilot group.

**Watch for the specific trap of "we're communicating a lot but nothing's moving"** — this usually means Stickiness is weak (the message isn't the kind that prompts action, no matter how often it's repeated) or Law of the Few is weak (the communication is broadcasting through official channels, not through people others actually trust and follow). More volume through the same channel rarely fixes either.

### Step 3: Output

Concise and direct — practitioner audience:

1. **Factor-by-factor diagnosis**: one line each (e.g. "Law of the Few: Weak — no named Connectors/Mavens engaged, relying on hierarchy" / "Stickiness: Developing — clear ask, but not yet tested with real audiences" / "Context: Strong — small-unit rollout, environment redesigned to support new behavior")
2. **Where to concentrate energy**: name the weakest factor(s) explicitly as the highest-leverage focus, not a balanced push across all three
3. **Recommendations** — specific and tied to what the user actually described (e.g. name the type of person to recruit, the specific message element to sharpen, the specific environmental cue to remove), not generic advice
4. **Rationale** — briefly tie back to why that factor matters (from Background) so it reads as reasoned advice

### Step 4: Memo version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo, slide snippet, or client-ready version, reformat into a short structured document (headers: Factor-by-Factor Diagnosis / Where to Concentrate Energy / Recommended Actions). Keep it to roughly a half page to one slide. Use the docx or pptx skill if they want an actual file.

## Concentration Playbook (target the specific weak factor, don't dump all of these)

**If Law of the Few is weak:** Stop relying on the org chart to do a network's job. Map who people actually go to for information and who they actually trust to tell them something's worth doing — these are rarely the same people as the formal project team. Recruit specific Connectors, Mavens, and Salesmen deliberately and give them real material (not just talking points) to work with.

**If Stickiness is weak:** The problem usually isn't repetition, it's specificity. Sharpen the ask into something concrete enough to act on immediately, test it with a small real audience before broadcasting further, and redesign the format/packaging rather than just resending the same message through more channels.

**If Context is weak:** Look for the smallest visible signals of the old way still present and remove them first — they undercut new messaging faster than people expect. If the rollout is organized as one large undifferentiated group, break it into smaller units where mutual accountability can actually operate. Check timing and competing priorities honestly; a well-designed change launched into a context already saturated with other asks will struggle regardless of message quality.

**If multiple factors are weak:** Don't spread effort evenly across all three — pick the single weakest and fix it first. A change with no Connectors/Mavens/Salesmen engaged won't spread no matter how sticky the message or how well-designed the context, so Law of the Few weakness usually deserves priority if it's one of the factors flagged.

## Notes

- This is about spread mechanics for a change already underway with some traction, not initial buy-in from a standing start — if the user is asking about whether people feel urgency at all, that's closer to Kotter Stage 1 or the Persuasion framework's Framing stage.
- Don't let a strong Context or Stickiness score compensate for a weak Law of the Few — Gladwell's research treats named individuals as closer to a necessary condition for spread than an optional enhancer.
- This pairs with the other change diagnostic skills in this project: DICE predicts *whether* a project will succeed given its setup; Technical/Adaptive diagnoses *what kind* of change it is; Theory E/O diagnoses *why* it's happening; Persuasion helps *build the case*; Six Steps and Kotter diagnose *how far the change has progressed*; this one diagnoses *where to concentrate effort so it actually spreads*, and pairs especially closely with Network Position (identifying who specifically holds influence) once built.
