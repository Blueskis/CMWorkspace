# Kotter's Dual Operating System — Keeping the Day Job Running

Diagnoses whether an organization has stood up a genuine second operating system for strategic change — a network structure running in parallel with, not instead of or bolted onto, the hierarchy — using John Kotter's model from "Accelerate: Building Strategic Agility for a Faster-Moving World."

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

Kotter's core argument: a single hierarchical operating system is good at what it's built for — reliable, efficient, low-risk execution of business as usual — and structurally bad at fast strategic change, because hierarchies are optimized for control and predictability, not speed and adaptability. Trying to run large-scale change entirely through the hierarchy tends to produce one of two failure modes: change stalls under the hierarchy's normal risk-averse decision cycles, or change work crowds out the day job because the same people and structures are asked to do both. Kotter's answer isn't to replace the hierarchy — it's to run a **second operating system**, a more agile, network-like structure, alongside it. Five characteristics define a genuine second system, not just a task force with a new name:

1. **Many change agents, not a few assigned** — a broad network of people pulled from across levels and functions, not a small dedicated team asked to carry change for everyone else.
2. **"Want to," not "have to"** — participants are volunteers, drawn by a genuine opportunity or sense of purpose, not assigned to the initiative as another line on their job description.
3. **Head and heart, not just head** — the network engages people emotionally as well as rationally; pure business-case logic recruits compliance, not the energy a real network runs on.
4. **Leadership, not just management** — the network is run through vision, opportunity, and inspiration (leadership), not budgets, org charts, and task assignment (management) — those still belong to the hierarchy.
5. **Tightly connected to the hierarchy — two systems, one organization** — the network has real information flow, resourcing, and legitimacy from the hierarchy, and feeds results back into it. It is not a rogue skunkworks operating in isolation, and it is not quietly reabsorbed into the hierarchy's normal reporting lines either.

**The core diagnostic question this skill answers**: is strategic change being attempted through the hierarchy alone (risking day-to-day operations being crowded out, or change stalling on slow decision cycles), through a network that's disconnected from the hierarchy (risking no real resourcing, legitimacy, or follow-through), or through a genuine dual system where both run in parallel and reinforce each other?

## Process

### Step 1: Run the checklist

Present the checklist below, or extract the same information from a narrative description if that's how the user prefers to describe the situation — ask only about genuine gaps. Always include an "Others" free-text line.

**Existence of a network structure**
1. There's an identifiable group of people working on this change who sit outside the formal reporting line for it — not just the assigned project team
2. This group spans multiple levels and functions, not concentrated in one department or level

**"Want to," not "have to"**
3. People in the network chose to be involved — nominated themselves or were invited and accepted, rather than being assigned as a job responsibility
4. Involvement is understood as additive/voluntary, not something that shows up as a mandatory line item in someone's formal role

**Head and heart**
5. People in the network can articulate a personal, felt reason this matters to them, not just the business case
6. There's visible energy and initiative from the network beyond what's formally asked of them

**Led, not managed**
7. The network is guided through vision, purpose, and opportunity — not run via task assignment, status reporting, and budget control the way the hierarchy runs
8. Network participants have real latitude to shape how they pursue the opportunity, not just execute assigned tasks

**Connected to the hierarchy**
9. The network has real resourcing and visible legitimacy from senior leadership — not operating on goodwill and borrowed time alone
10. Information flows both ways — the network's findings and progress genuinely reach and influence the hierarchy, and the hierarchy's constraints and priorities reach the network
11. The hierarchy's normal operations (the day job) are protected — people in the network still have functioning capacity for their regular roles, not silently overloaded
12. The network isn't simply being reabsorbed into normal reporting lines and losing its distinct, faster-moving character

*Others* — free text for anything not captured above; classify it yourself against the five characteristics, stating your reasoning in one line.

### Step 2: Classify the setup

Score each of the five characteristics as its own pillar rather than only checking two coarse buckets — a real network can be strong on most pillars and weak on just one, and forcing that into the same category as a network that's weak across the board mischaracterizes what's actually working and misdirects the recommendation.

- **Pillar A — Network exists** (items 1-2)
- **Pillar B — Want to, not have to** (items 3-4)
- **Pillar C — Head and heart** (items 5-6)
- **Pillar D — Led, not managed** (items 7-8)
- **Pillar E — Connected to hierarchy** (items 9-12)

**Pillar A mostly unticked:** → **Hierarchy-Only**. Change is being attempted entirely through the existing structure. Watch for two specific symptoms: change stalling on the hierarchy's normal risk-averse decision cycles, or the day job visibly suffering because the same people and structures are being asked to carry both.

**Pillar A present, Pillar E weak:** → **Disconnected Network**. A network-like structure exists but lacks real resourcing, legitimacy, or two-way connection to the hierarchy — at risk of becoming a rogue skunkworks that produces good ideas nobody acts on, or that quietly starves once initial enthusiasm fades.

**Pillar A present, Pillars B-D weak across the board, even if E is strong:** → **Task Force Wearing Network Clothing**. This is a common trap — a group exists and is well-connected to leadership, but participants were assigned rather than volunteered, and it's being managed (tasks, deadlines, status reports) rather than led (vision, opportunity). This will produce compliance, not the speed and energy Kotter's model depends on.

**Pillar A present, Pillar E present, Pillars B-D mostly strong:** → **Genuine Dual System**. Confirm this is being actively sustained, not just true at initial launch — dual systems tend to erode toward one of the above failure modes over time without deliberate attention.

**Pillar A present, Pillar E present, most of B-D strong but exactly one pillar (most often B, "want to") is clearly weak:** → **Genuine Dual System with a Flagged Gap on [that pillar]**. Don't collapse this into Task Force Wearing Network Clothing — that would mischaracterize the pillars that are genuinely working (e.g. real leadership and real hierarchy connection) and point the recommendation at rebuilding a structure that doesn't need rebuilding. Instead, name the specific weak pillar and treat it as a targeted fix: for a "want to" gap specifically, that usually means offering a graceful opt-out to genuinely unwilling participants, backfilling with real volunteers where willingness is low, and helping those who stay find their own personal reason to be there rather than relying on whatever trait (vocal, expert, influential) got them selected in the first place.

### Step 3: Output

Concise and direct — practitioner audience:

1. **Classification** (from Step 2) with which specific items drove the call
2. **What's actually at risk** given the classification — named specifically (e.g. "day job capacity is being silently consumed" vs. "network has energy but no resourcing to act on it")
3. **Recommendations** — from the playbook below, targeted at the specific classification and weak items, not a generic push toward "build more of a network"

### Step 4: Memo version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo or steering committee summary, reformat into a short structured document (headers: Classification / Key Risk / Recommended Actions). Keep it to roughly a half page to one slide. Use the docx or pptx skill if they want an actual file.

## Recommendation Playbook

**If Hierarchy-Only:** Don't add more people or hours to the existing structure — stand up an actual network: identify volunteers (not assignees) from across levels and functions, give them a real opportunity to lead a piece of the change with genuine latitude, and explicitly protect their day-job capacity rather than assuming the change work is "extra."

**If Disconnected Network:** The energy exists but needs a hierarchy connection to translate into anything durable. Get explicit resourcing and visible senior sponsorship, and build a real two-way information channel — a standing update to leadership that actually changes decisions, not a status report that goes into a folder.

**If Task Force Wearing Network Clothing:** Convert assignment into invitation — ask who wants to be involved rather than assigning it, and shift from managing tasks to leading around a shared opportunity. This is often a mindset and facilitation change more than a structural one; the same people can become a real network if engaged differently.

**If Genuine Dual System with a Flagged Gap:** Resist the urge to treat this like Task Force Wearing Network Clothing — don't rework the leadership style, latitude, or hierarchy connection if those pillars are genuinely working; that would fix what isn't broken and risk unsettling it. Target the specific weak pillar only. For a "want to" gap specifically: offer a graceful opt-out for participants who were assigned and genuinely don't want to be there, backfill with real volunteers where willingness is low, and invest in helping those who stay connect to their own personal reason for being involved rather than leaning on the trait (vocal, expert, influential) that got them selected. Watch for uneven energy across participants as the practical symptom of this gap — the willing carry real momentum while the unwilling go through the motions or, worse, represent the change poorly to their own teams.

**If Genuine Dual System:** Protect what's working — this tends to erode as initial energy fades or as the hierarchy tries to reabsorb the network into normal reporting lines for the sake of tidiness. Recommend periodically re-checking all five characteristics, especially "want to, not have to" and day-job protection, since those degrade first.

## Notes

- A dual operating system is not the same thing as a project team or steering committee — the differentiator is the five characteristics together, especially voluntary participation and being led rather than managed. A well-resourced, well-connected assigned task force is still Hierarchy-Only in spirit even if it looks like a network on an org chart.
- This pairs with Tipping Point's Law of the Few and Network Position — the network this skill diagnoses is exactly the kind of structure that needs real Connectors, Mavens, and Salesmen in it to function, and Network Position helps identify who those people specifically are. It also pairs with Kotter's 8-Step model broadly, since Kotter's "Accelerate" reframes the 8 steps as continuous work the network does, rather than a one-time sequence the hierarchy executes.
