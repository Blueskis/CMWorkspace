# Beer, Eisenstat & Spector — Six Steps to Effective Change

Diagnoses where a change initiative genuinely stands using the task-alignment model from "Why Change Programs Don't Produce Change" (HBR, 1990), and flags the specific failure pattern the article is most known for warning against.

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

The six steps, in sequence:
1. **Mobilize commitment** through joint diagnosis of business problems — leadership and employees together, grounded in concrete performance issues, not a mandate from the top.
2. **Develop a shared vision** of how to organize and manage differently — concrete roles, responsibilities, and working relationships, shaped with input from those who'll live it.
3. **Foster consensus, competence, and cohesion** — real buy-in (not just compliance), the actual skills to work the new way, and teams whose working relationships support it.
4. **Spread revitalization** to other units — critically, this should happen organically as units see what worked elsewhere and adapt it to their context, *not* be pushed top-down as an identical mandate.
5. **Institutionalize** through formal policies, systems, and structures (hiring, promotion, performance management) — but only *after* the new behaviors are already working informally.
6. **Monitor and adjust** — an active, honest feedback loop that actually changes the approach based on what's being learned.

**The core warning from the original article**: most corporate change programs fail not because the six steps are wrong, but because organizations skip ahead — especially jumping to Step 5 (formal policies, org redesign, new HR systems) or forcing Step 4 (mandating the same model everywhere) before Steps 1-3 have genuinely taken hold. This produces change that looks complete on paper (new structure, new policy, new comms) while the underlying behavior hasn't actually shifted. This is the single most damaging and most common failure mode to watch for.

## Process

### Step 1: Intake — checklist + open-ended, per step

For each of the six steps, ask the checklist items (tick what's genuinely true) and the open-ended questions (to capture real context, since self-reported completion often overstates progress). Present all six together if the user seems ready to answer at length, or work through them one at a time — use judgment.

**Step 1 — Mobilize Commitment (joint diagnosis)**
Checklist:
- Business unit leaders and employees have jointly diagnosed the problems driving this change, not just leadership alone
- The diagnosis is grounded in concrete performance issues (margins, quality, competitiveness), not an abstract mandate
- Key stakeholders across levels agree on what the actual problem is
- There's real urgency behind solving it, not just leadership concern

Open-ended: What specific business problem triggered this? Who was involved in diagnosing it? How aligned are people on what the problem actually is?

**Step 2 — Develop Shared Vision**
Checklist:
- There's a clear vision for how the organization/unit should work differently
- The vision addresses concrete roles, responsibilities, and working relationships, not just aspirational language
- The vision was shaped with input from those who'll live it, not handed down fully formed
- People can describe the future state in practical terms

Open-ended: What does the new way of working actually look like? Who helped shape it? Can people describe it concretely, or is it still abstract?

**Step 3 — Foster Consensus, Competence, Cohesion**
Checklist:
- Broad consensus exists for the vision, not just formal sign-off
- People have, or are actively building, the skills needed to work the new way
- Teams show real cohesion — working relationships support the new model, not just individual understanding of it
- Resistance is being worked through, not overridden or ignored

Open-ended: Where does consensus feel real vs. where is it just compliance? What skill gaps remain? How are teams actually working together under the new model so far?

**Step 4 — Spread Revitalization (without pushing from the top)**
Checklist:
- Practices from early-adopting units are spreading to other parts of the organization
- Spread is happening organically/through peer influence, not top-down mandate
- Local units have room to adapt the approach to their own context, not forced into an identical template
- Leadership is enabling spread (removing barriers, sharing what worked) rather than dictating it

Open-ended: Where has this already spread on its own, and where hasn't it? Is leadership pushing this top-down, or supporting organic adoption? What's stopping other units from adopting it yet?

**Step 5 — Institutionalize (formal policies, systems, structures)**
Checklist:
- Formal policies, systems, and structures (org design, performance management, promotion criteria) have been updated to reinforce the new way of working
- The change is embedded in hiring, promotion, and evaluation, not just described in a memo
- Formalization happened *after* the new behaviors were already working informally
- The new formal systems reflect what was actually learned in Steps 1-4, not a generic template applied from outside

Open-ended: What formal systems/policies have actually changed? Did formalization happen after real behavior change, or was it announced first? Do the new systems match what's actually been working?

**Step 6 — Monitor and Adjust**
Checklist:
- There's an active mechanism for tracking how the change is really going, not just whether it was "rolled out"
- Leadership is genuinely willing to adjust the approach based on what's being learned
- Problems surfacing during the change are being addressed, not suppressed or ignored
- There's a regular cadence for revisiting and course-correcting

Open-ended: How are you tracking whether this is actually working? What's been adjusted so far? What problems have surfaced, and how were they handled?

**If the user gives a narrative instead of working through checklist/open-ended per step**, extract what you can for each step, state your read explicitly, and only ask for what's genuinely missing.

**Always present findings in step order (1 through 6), never reordered by confidence or by how much evidence is available.** It's tempting to lead with the steps you have the strongest read on and leave the step needing a follow-up question for last, but that obscures the sequence — and sequence is the entire point of this framework. Even mid-conversation, before the full picture is assembled, walk through steps in order and mark the ones still pending rather than skipping ahead to steps with richer evidence.

### Step 2: Score and find the true bottleneck

Per step, score checklist completion: 4/4 = **Complete**, 2-3/4 = **Partial**, 0-1/4 = **Not Started**. Use open-ended answers to sanity-check — self-reported ticks often overstate progress; trust the substance if it contradicts the tick count.

**Find the genuine current step**: working from Step 1 forward, this is the *first* step that is not Complete. This is where the real gap is, regardless of what activity exists at later steps.

**Check for the skip pattern**: if Step 4 (spread) or Step 5 (institutionalize) shows Partial or Complete activity while an earlier step (1-3) is not yet Complete, flag this explicitly and prominently — this is premature institutionalization or forced spread, the classic failure mode. Be especially alert if Step 5 shows activity while Steps 1-3 are not Complete — this is the single most damaging version of the pattern, since it means formal systems are locking in a change that hasn't actually taken root behaviorally.

**Step 6 is exempt from the skip-pattern check.** Monitoring and adjusting is inherently an ongoing, parallel activity in the original framework, not a sequential gate — early check-ins or informal course-correction don't lock anything in prematurely the way forced spread or premature formalization do. Seeing Step 6 activity ahead of "schedule" is healthy, not a risk signal.

### Step 3: Output

Concise and direct — practitioner audience:

1. **Step-by-step status, in order 1 through 6**: one line each (Complete / Partial / Not Started)
2. **Genuine current step** — named explicitly, with what's missing to complete it
3. **Skip-pattern flag** if applicable — named explicitly, with the specific risk (change looks official but hasn't taken root)
4. **Recommendations** — focused on solidifying the genuine current step first; if a skip pattern was found, explicitly recommend addressing that before continuing further formalization/spread, even if that means pausing later-step activity already underway
5. **Rationale** — tie back to why sequence matters here (from Background), so it reads as reasoned advice, not just a status report

### Step 4: Memo version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo or steering committee summary, reformat into a short structured document (headers: Step-by-Step Status / Genuine Current Step / Skip-Pattern Risk (if any) / Recommended Actions). Keep it to roughly a half page to one page. Use the docx or pptx skill if they want an actual file.

## Notes

- Don't let visible activity at later steps (a new policy, a formal rollout) be mistaken for progress if earlier steps aren't genuinely complete — that's exactly the mistake this framework exists to catch.
- A user's own sense of how far along they are ("we're basically done, just formalizing now") is exactly the kind of self-assessment this skill should test against the actual checklist/open-ended substance, not accept at face value.
- This pairs with the other change diagnostic skills: DICE predicts *whether* a project will succeed given its setup; Technical/Adaptive diagnoses *what kind* of change it is; Theory E/O diagnoses *why* it's happening; Persuasion helps *build the case*; this one diagnoses *how far the change has actually progressed* and whether the sequence is being respected.
