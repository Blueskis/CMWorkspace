# Kegan & Lahey's Immunity to Change — Why Genuine Commitment Isn't Producing Behavior Change

Diagnoses why a specific person (or small persona/group) who authentically wants a change to succeed keeps behaving in ways that undermine it, using Robert Kegan and Lisa Lahey's Immunity to Change framework ("How the Way We Talk Can Change the Way We Work"). Produces a 4-column map that makes the hidden logic visible, and a concrete, low-risk next step to test it.

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

Kegan & Lahey's core insight: when someone genuinely wants to change but doesn't, it's rarely a willpower problem — it's usually because they're running an unconscious "immune system" that's just as committed to *preventing* the change as they consciously are to achieving it, because the change threatens something the immune system is protecting them from. The map has four columns, built in order:

1. **Column 1 — Improvement Goal**: the specific, genuine goal or behavior the person says they want (and the change genuinely requires of them). Must be authentic, not imposed — if they don't actually want it, this framework doesn't apply; that's ordinary resistance or disagreement instead.
2. **Column 2 — Doing/Not Doing Instead**: the specific, observable behaviors that work against Column 1. Concrete actions (or concrete avoidances), not vague traits — "doesn't delegate the budget review" not "isn't a team player."
3. **Column 3 — Hidden Competing Commitment**: surfaced by asking what would feel uncomfortable, risky, or worrying about doing the opposite of Column 2 (i.e., actually doing the Column 1 behavior consistently). The worry reveals a competing commitment, usually phrased as "I am also committed to *not* [experiencing X]" or "I am also committed to [protecting Y]." This commitment is just as real and just as sensible from the inside as the stated goal — the person isn't being irrational, they're protecting something that matters to them.
4. **Column 4 — Big Assumption**: the unexamined belief that makes the competing commitment feel necessary. Usually takes the form "I assume that if [Column 1 behavior], then [bad outcome] will happen." This assumption is what actually needs to be tested — not argued with, not overridden by willpower, but checked against reality through a small, safe, real-world test.

**Why this differs from ordinary resistance**: resistance says "I don't want this" or "I disagree this is the right change." Immunity says "I genuinely want this, and I'm still not doing it" — the gap between authentic intention and actual behavior is the signal this framework is built for. If in doubt during intake, check this explicitly before building the map.

**Use plain language with the user — never say "Column 1/2/3/4."** The column structure above is Kegan & Lahey's internal book terminology, useful for keeping your own thinking organized, but it's jargon to someone who hasn't read the book, even a fluent change management practitioner. Always talk about and label the four parts in plain terms instead:

- Column 1 (Improvement Goal) → **"What good looks like"**
- Column 2 (Doing/Not Doing Instead) → **"What's happening instead"**
- Column 3 (Hidden Competing Commitment) → **"What they're actually protecting"**
- Column 4 (Big Assumption) → **"The belief driving it"**

## Process

### Step 1: Confirm this is genuine immunity, not resistance

Before building a map, check: does this person actually want the change to succeed, or are they opposed/skeptical/disagreeing with it? If the honest answer is disagreement or opposition, say so plainly and point toward a different tool — Persuasion (building the case) or Theory E/O (understanding what's driving the change) are more likely to help than a 4-column map, which assumes good faith and stalls if forced onto someone who isn't genuinely on board.

### Step 2: Get specific about what good looks like

Ask: what specific behavior does this person (or persona/group) need to consistently demonstrate for this change to succeed? Push for something concrete and observable, not a value or trait. If the user gives something abstract ("be more strategic"), ask what that specific behavior would actually look like in practice.

### Step 3: Get specific about what's happening instead

Ask: what does this person actually do, or avoid doing, that works against what good looks like? Again, push for specific, observable instances rather than a general impression. If the user only has a general impression ("they just don't seem engaged"), ask for a recent concrete example.

### Step 4: Surface what they're actually protecting

This is the step that requires the most care, since the answer usually isn't obvious to the person doing it (or to the user describing them from outside) at first pass. Ask a version of: "Imagine this person did the opposite of that — consistently did what good looks like, starting now. What about that would feel uncomfortable, risky, or worrying, to them?" Take the worry and reframe it as something they're protecting: "So it sounds like they might also be trying to protect [X]." Offer this framing back and let the user confirm, adjust, or go deeper — this is inherently an interpretive step, so hold it as a hypothesis, not a fact, until it resonates.

**If the user is mapping themselves rather than someone else, this step tends to work better and faster** — self-report of the worry is more reliable than someone else's guess at another person's inner experience. If the user is mapping someone else (a stakeholder, a boss, a peer), be explicit that this and the next step are informed hypotheses based on observed behavior, not certain facts, and that the real test is whether they resonate if ever checked with the person directly.

### Step 5: Surface the belief driving it

Ask: "What does this person seem to assume would happen if they stopped protecting [that thing]? What's the feared consequence they're taking as a given?" This should surface as an assumption stated as if it were fact ("If I push back on my boss's plan, I'll be seen as not a team player and passed over for promotion") — the goal is to state it explicitly enough that it becomes checkable, not left as vague dread.

### Step 6: Design a small, safe test

The point of the map isn't insight for its own sake — it's to design a low-risk, low-stakes, real-world test of that belief, small enough that even if the fear comes true, the cost is minor, but real enough to actually generate evidence. A good test:
- Targets the belief directly, not just "try harder" at what good looks like
- Is small and safe enough to actually attempt, not a leap to the full desired behavior all at once
- Has a way to observe and honestly evaluate what actually happens afterward
- Is something the person (or the user, on their behalf) can genuinely commit to trying

Propose 1-2 specific test ideas, tailored to the actual content of the map — not a generic "try being more open" suggestion.

### Step 7: Output

Present the completed map clearly in plain language, followed by the recommended test:

1. **What good looks like**
2. **What's happening instead**
3. **What they're actually protecting**
4. **The belief driving it**
5. **Recommended small test**, with what it would tell you depending on the outcome
6. Note explicitly wherever items 3-4 are hypotheses rather than confirmed, and suggest checking them directly with the person if that's feasible and appropriate

### Step 8: Memo version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo or coaching brief, reformat into a short structured document (headers: The Map / Hidden Logic / Recommended Test / What to Watch For). Keep it to roughly a half page. This is often more sensitive than other change diagnostics since it involves a named individual's inner motivations — remind the user to handle it with appropriate discretion if it's going into a written document.

## Notes

- This framework is about one person or a tightly-defined persona at a time. If several different "key people" are stuck for different reasons, build separate maps rather than forcing them into one — two people can share the same "what good looks like" and still be protecting completely different things.
- Don't let the map become a way to pathologize or blame the person — the competing commitment is protecting something legitimate from their point of view; the goal is to test the assumption, not to argue them out of caring about it.
- This pairs with the other change diagnostic skills in this project: DICE predicts *whether* a project will succeed given its setup; Technical/Adaptive diagnoses *what kind* of change it is; Theory E/O diagnoses *why* it's happening; Persuasion helps *build the case*; Six Steps and Kotter diagnose *how far the change has progressed*; Tipping Point diagnoses *where to concentrate spread effort*. This one is the one to reach for specifically when the blocker is a specific person whose stated commitment and actual behavior don't match — it goes a level deeper than group-level diagnostics.
