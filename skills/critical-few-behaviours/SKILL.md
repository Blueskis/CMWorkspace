# McKinsey Influence Model — Critical Few Behaviours

Identifies the small set of specific, observable behaviors a change actually depends on, checks whether all four influence levers are reinforcing them consistently, and finds the highest-leverage gaps — using McKinsey's Influence Model (Keller & Aiken, "The Inconvenient Truth About Change Management"; Katzenbach's "critical few" concept).

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

McKinsey's research finding: successful large-scale behavior change is rarely the product of broad, diffuse effort across many initiatives — it comes from identifying a small number (typically 3-5) of specific, observable "critical few" behaviors that matter most, and then hitting *those specific behaviors* with all four influence levers simultaneously and consistently:

1. **Role Modeling** — leaders and visible influencers demonstrably do the behavior themselves, in view of the people who need to adopt it. "Do as I say" without "do as I do" is one of the most common and most damaging gaps.
2. **Fostering Understanding & Conviction** — people genuinely understand why this specific behavior matters (not just the change in the abstract) and believe in it, engaging both the rational case and an emotional connection to why it matters, not mere awareness or compliance.
3. **Developing Talent & Skills** — people actually have the skill, confidence, and practice to perform the specific behavior, not just the intention. A behavior people believe in but can't yet competently do will stall regardless of conviction.
4. **Reinforcing with Formal Mechanisms** — structures, processes, metrics, and incentives visibly reward the new behavior and don't quietly continue to reward the old one. A mismatch here (old KPIs still rewarding the old behavior) undermines everything else.

**The core diagnostic insight**: change efforts often fail not from lacking any of these four levers in general, but from spreading them across too many behaviors too thinly, or applying only 1-2 levers to a given behavior while assuming that's enough. A behavior reinforced by only Role Modeling and Understanding, with no Skill-building or Formal Mechanism support, will produce believers who still can't or don't act differently day to day.

## Facilitation Stance

The consultant using this skill is a professional who often already has good instincts about their own situation — your job is frequently to ask the question that lets them arrive at the diagnosis themselves, not to be the one who always supplies it.

Don't default to prescriptive recommendations as your first move whenever a gap surfaces. During the diagnostic and exploration steps especially (identifying behaviors, building the grid, discussing what's driving a lever), notice moments where a well-chosen open question — "what do you think is actually driving that gap?" or "if you had to guess why Formal Mechanisms is missing here, what would you say?" — invites the consultant to reason through their own strategy, and reach for that before reaching for the playbook. Save direct, specific recommendations for when the consultant is genuinely stuck, explicitly asks what you'd suggest, or when compiling a final Step 4 output or requested memo, where a client-ready deliverable needs a stated recommendation rather than an open question. This isn't about withholding expertise — when asked directly, answer directly and specifically. It's about not rushing past the consultant's own thinking to get there.

## Process

### Step 1: Identify the critical few behaviors

Open by inviting the consultant to describe the current situation and pain points in their own words, rather than cold-asking for a pre-formed list of 3-5 behaviors — most consultants arrive with a problem, not a behavior inventory already sorted. Listen for what's actually going wrong, then propose candidate critical-few behaviors drawn from that narrative, stated explicitly ("Based on what you've described, here's my read of the behaviors that might be the real crux...") and invite the consultant to confirm, adjust, or replace them.

If the narrative or a direct answer offers something abstract ("be more collaborative," "buy-in," "motivation"), treat that as a driver or symptom worth exploring, not itself a behavior — but per the Facilitation Stance above, try asking the consultant what the concrete version looks like to them before supplying your own translation. Only offer your own reading if they're stuck or ask you to.

**If more than 5 behaviors emerge**, note explicitly that spreading reinforcement across too many is itself a common failure mode, and ask the consultant which behaviors, if consistently adopted, would drive the most value — let them do the prioritizing rather than deciding for them.

### Step 2: Build the behaviors-by-levers grid

For each critical-few behavior identified in Step 1, assess all four levers. Ask open-ended questions per lever, per behavior — this is more effective as a grid walkthrough than a flat checklist, since the same lever can be strong for one behavior and absent for another:

- **Role Modeling**: Who visibly does this specific behavior themselves, and how visible is it to the people who need to adopt it? Can you name a specific recent instance?
- **Understanding & Conviction**: Do people understand specifically why *this* behavior matters (not just the change overall)? Is there an emotional case, not just a rational one?
- **Talent & Skills**: Do people actually have the skill and practice to do this specific behavior competently, or just the intention?
- **Formal Mechanisms**: What metrics, processes, or incentives currently touch this behavior — and do any existing formal mechanisms still reward the *old* behavior instead?

### Step 3: Score and find the gaps

For each behavior, mark each of the 4 levers as **Present** (clear, specific evidence), **Weak** (some effort but thin or inconsistent), or **Absent**. Build a simple grid (behaviors as rows, the 4 levers as columns) so gaps are visible at a glance.

**Prioritize gaps by**:
1. Behaviors with the fewest levers Present — these are the most under-reinforced and least likely to stick
2. Any behavior missing Formal Mechanisms specifically, or worse, where a formal mechanism is actively rewarding the *old* behavior — this is usually the single most damaging gap, since people rationally follow what they're measured and rewarded on regardless of what they're told
3. Any behavior missing Role Modeling where senior/visible people are asking others to do something they don't visibly do themselves — this undermines Understanding & Conviction even when messaging is otherwise strong

### Step 4: Output

Concise and direct — practitioner audience:

1. **The critical few behaviors**, listed explicitly and concretely
2. **The grid** — behaviors × levers, with Present/Weak/Absent per cell
3. **Priority gaps** — named specifically (which behavior, which lever), ranked by the logic in Step 3
4. **Recommendations** — targeted at closing the specific highest-priority gaps, using the lever playbook below, not a generic push across all four levers for all behaviors

This compiled output is the one place a direct, stated recommendation is usually the right call even under the Facilitation Stance above — it's the deliverable, not the exploration. That said, if the conversation has already surfaced the consultant's own strategy for a given gap, reflect that back as theirs rather than restating the playbook's generic version.

### Step 5: Memo version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo or client-ready version, reformat into a short structured document with the grid as a visible table (headers: Critical Few Behaviors / Reinforcement Grid / Priority Gaps / Recommended Actions). Use the docx, pptx, or xlsx skill if they want an actual file with a real table — a grid like this is often clearer as a small spreadsheet or slide than plain text.

## Lever Playbook (target the specific gap, don't dump all of these — and per the Facilitation Stance, reach for this when the consultant is stuck, asks directly, or you're compiling Step 4/a memo, not as the automatic first response to every gap)

**If Role Modeling is missing for a behavior:** Identify specific, visible leaders or influencers (not necessarily the most senior person — see the tipping-point and network-position skills for identifying who actually has reach) and get them to demonstrably do the behavior in a setting where the target audience will see it. A private commitment from a leader who never visibly acts on it doesn't count.

**If Understanding & Conviction is missing:** Don't just repeat the message — connect this specific behavior to something the audience already cares about, and make the case both rationally (why it works) and emotionally (why it matters to them specifically). Test whether people can restate why *this particular behavior* matters, not just the change's slogan.

**If Talent & Skills is missing:** Conviction without capability produces frustration, not behavior change. Build real practice opportunities — not a single training session, but repeated, low-stakes chances to try the behavior with feedback, until it's not effortful anymore.

**If Formal Mechanisms is missing, or actively contradicts the behavior:** This is usually the highest-priority fix. Audit what's actually being measured, reviewed, and rewarded, and change what's misaligned — even one visibly-corrected metric or process can do more than additional messaging on the other three levers. People trust what they're measured on more than what they're told.

## Notes

- All four levers reinforcing the same specific behavior is the goal — a change with excellent Understanding & Conviction but no Formal Mechanisms, or vice versa, is a partial effort even if it looks robust on paper.
- Resist letting the user add more than 3-5 behaviors to track with real intensity — breadth is the enemy of this framework's core insight. More behaviors tracked loosely is worse than fewer tracked rigorously.
- This pairs with the other change diagnostic skills in this project: Kotter Stage 8 and Six Steps' Institutionalize step both touch formal-mechanism-style anchoring at the whole-initiative level, while this skill operates at the level of specific named behaviors — useful together when the initiative-level diagnostic says "not yet anchored" and you need to get concrete about exactly which behaviors and which levers are the gap. Tipping Point's Law of the Few and this skill's Role Modeling lever both depend on identifying who actually has influence — Network Position is the tool for finding those specific people.
