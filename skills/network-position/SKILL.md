# Network Position — Who You Need in Your Corner

Maps the specific people a change effort depends on by their position in the informal network, not just their formal title, crosses that against their current sentiment toward the change, and produces a prioritized list of who to engage first and how.

## Background (for your own calibration, don't dump this on the user)

Organizational network analysis' core insight: the people whose support most determines whether a change sustains are often not the people with the most senior titles — they're the people with the most reach or trust in the actual, informal network of who talks to whom and whose opinion carries weight. Four position types matter most for sustaining change:

- **Central Connectors / Hubs**: people with an unusually large number of direct working relationships — when they're for or against something, a lot of people hear about it fast.
- **Brokers / Boundary Spanners**: people who bridge otherwise-disconnected groups (sitting in a "structural hole" between silos) — critical for a change to spread across group boundaries rather than staying siloed in one part of the organization.
- **Peripheral-but-critical specialists**: people with few connections overall, but who hold specific expertise or control a specific process the change depends on — easy to overlook because they're not central, but capable of quietly stalling a change if not engaged.
- **Energizers vs. De-energizers**: people whose interactions leave others more engaged and motivated (Energizers) versus drained and deflated (De-energizers) — this is somewhat independent of formal position, and a well-connected De-energizer can do real damage to sustained momentum even while nominally "supporting" the change.

**The formal-vs-informal authority mismatch is the single most useful thing this analysis surfaces**: a skeptical VP with an impressive title but little real informal network reach may matter less to whether a change sustains than a skeptical senior analyst who happens to be a Hub or Broker. Formal org charts systematically miss this, which is exactly why network position is worth mapping explicitly rather than assumed from titles.

## Process

### Step 1: Identify candidates by position type

Ask the user to name specific people (by name or role, whatever they're comfortable sharing) who plausibly fit each position type. This works best as an open conversation rather than a rigid checklist, since the whole point is surfacing people who wouldn't show up on a formal stakeholder list:

- Who do a lot of different people go to, or naturally end up talking to, across the organization? (candidate Hubs)
- Who has a foot in more than one team, function, or level, and is trusted by both sides? (candidate Brokers)
- Who holds specific expertise or controls a specific process/system this change depends on, even if they're not particularly central otherwise? (candidate Peripheral specialists)
- Who do people leave a conversation with feeling more energized and willing to engage — and, separately, who has the opposite effect, even if they're nominally supportive? (Energizers / De-energizers)

Don't force every named person into exactly one category — some people are Hubs *and* Energizers, and that combination matters. If the user's description hints at a combination without confirming it (e.g. they mention someone is trusted and reaches across functions, and separately that people leave interactions energized, without saying whether that's the same person or a property of a format/setting), don't let it stay fuzzy — ask directly whether it's the same person, since confirming a multi-category combination meaningfully strengthens (or changes) the priority read.

**If the user genuinely doesn't know who fits a category** — not uncommon, especially early in an engagement, before the consultant has their own read on the informal network — don't just record the gap and move on. Offer a concrete way to find out: ask them to find out who people would actually go to if they had a real concern about the change, rather than who's the official contact. That question surfaces genuine informal Hubs, Brokers, and quiet Skeptics far more reliably than asking directly "who's skeptical" or "who's well-connected," since people are more candid about who they trust than who they doubt.

### Step 2: Assess current sentiment and formal authority for each

For each person identified, capture:
- **Sentiment**: Advocate / Neutral / Skeptic / Unknown (be honest about Unknown rather than guessing — it's a real and useful category, since not knowing where a high-position person stands is itself a risk worth flagging)
- **Formal authority**: roughly how much positional/hierarchical authority they hold — this is captured specifically to check for a mismatch with their network position, not because formal authority is what matters most here

### Step 3: Build the priority matrix and flag mismatches

Cross network position against sentiment to prioritize:

- **Skeptic + Hub or Broker** → highest priority. Their position multiplies the reach of their skepticism; this is the person most likely to quietly slow or fragment the change if not engaged directly.
- **Unknown + Hub or Broker** → high priority to resolve. Not knowing where a highly-connected person stands is itself a risk — find out before assuming.
- **Advocate + Hub or Broker** → high priority to activate, not just leave alone. An engaged advocate with real network reach is one of the highest-leverage assets available; make sure they're actually being used, not just nominally supportive.
- **De-energizer, regardless of stated sentiment** → flag specifically, since even a nominal Advocate who drains energy in interactions can undermine sustained momentum in a way sentiment alone won't catch.
- **Peripheral specialist, any sentiment** → lower urgency for broad engagement, but flag explicitly if their specific expertise/process is a dependency for the change — a skeptical or disengaged specialist gatekeeping a critical dependency is a targeted risk even without broad network reach.

**Explicitly name any formal-vs-informal authority mismatch** — e.g., a skeptical senior leader with limited actual network reach may be lower priority than their title suggests, while a skeptical individual contributor with high informal reach may be higher priority than their title suggests. This mismatch is often the most useful single insight from the exercise.

### Step 4: Output

Concise and direct — practitioner audience:

1. **The matrix**: each named person/role, their position type(s), sentiment, and formal authority level — only rows for people actually identified. Don't add a placeholder row of "Unknown" values for a category nobody named; that misleadingly implies a specific person exists with unknown attributes. Instead, note unfilled categories as a short callout below the matrix (e.g. "No Skeptic or De-energizer identified — treat as an open risk, not a clean bill of health, and see the discovery technique in Step 1").
2. **Priority ranking**: who to engage first and why, using the logic in Step 3
3. **Formal/informal mismatches**: called out explicitly wherever the org chart would mislead
4. **Recommendations** — from the playbook below, tailored to each priority person's specific position-sentiment combination

### Step 5: Memo version (only if requested)

If asked for a memo or client-ready version, reformat into a short structured document with the matrix as a visible table (headers: Network Map / Priority Engagement List / Key Mismatches / Recommended Actions). Use the docx, pptx, or xlsx skill if they want an actual file — a matrix like this is often clearer as a small table or slide than plain text.

## Engagement Playbook

**Skeptic Hub/Broker:** Engage directly and early, one-on-one before broader rollout — don't let their skepticism reach the rest of the network unaddressed. Understand their specific objection genuinely (the Immunity to Change skill is useful here if their stated skepticism doesn't match what seems to actually be driving it) rather than just messaging past them.

**Unknown Hub/Broker:** Prioritize simply finding out where they stand — a direct conversation, before assuming either support or opposition.

**Advocate Hub/Broker:** Activate deliberately — give them real material, a specific role in spreading the change (see the Tipping Point skill's Law of the Few), and visible recognition, rather than assuming their support is already doing its full possible work passively.

**De-energizer:** Address the pattern directly regardless of their stated position — consider whether they need a different role in the change effort, coaching on how they show up in these conversations, or simply awareness that their impact doesn't match their intent.

**Peripheral specialist gatekeeping a dependency:** Engage specifically about their process/expertise dependency, even if broader network engagement isn't a priority for them — a narrow, targeted conversation about their specific piece is usually sufficient and more efficient than broad campaign-style engagement.

## Notes

- This is fundamentally about specific named people, not categories in the abstract — resist the temptation to answer generically ("engage your influencers") when the user hasn't actually named who those people are yet.
- Sentiment can and does shift — recommend re-checking the matrix periodically, especially for people flagged Unknown or for Skeptics after a targeted engagement attempt.
- This pairs with the other change diagnostic skills in this project: Tipping Point's Law of the Few identifies the *types* of people (Connectors, Mavens, Salesmen) needed for a change to spread — this skill is the tool for actually naming and prioritizing engagement with those specific people, especially once the initiative is further along and the question shifts from "how do we get this to spread" (Build phase) to "who do we need in our corner to sustain it" (Sustain phase). It also pairs with Dual Operating System, since a genuine network structure needs real Hubs and Brokers in it to function, and with Immunity to Change for understanding a specific high-priority Skeptic's underlying resistance in more depth.
