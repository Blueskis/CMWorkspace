> **This is a format exemplar with invented content. Delete before real use.**

## Why this block exists

Named reassurance stops more speculation than any amount of positive messaging. When people
learn something is changing, they fill the silence about everything adjacent to it — and what
they fill it with is worse than the truth roughly every time.

This block is cheap to write and disproportionately effective. It is also the first thing cut
under time pressure, which is why it lives in the bank rather than in someone's memory.

## The categories worth checking against every change

Run down this list with the practitioner at intake and keep the ones that apply. Most changes
touch two or three; stating those two or three explicitly is the whole win.

- **Pay and benefits** — amount, timing, method
- **Contract and terms** — hours, grade, entitlements
- **Reporting line** — who someone's manager is
- **Location and working pattern** — where and when people work
- **Job content** — what someone actually spends their day doing
- **Existing access** — systems and data people already use
- **In-flight work** — whether anything already submitted or approved is affected
- **Previous commitments** — bookings, requests and approvals already granted

## The shape

> **What's not changing**
>
> - [Category]: [the specific reassurance, stated positively]
> - [Category]: [...]

State it positively. "Your pay date is not moving" is weaker than "You'll continue to be paid
on the last working day of the month" — the negative construction leaves the alarming word
in the sentence.

## The rule that matters

**Never list something here you have not confirmed.** A reassurance that turns out to be wrong
costs more than the silence it was filling, and it is the single fastest way to lose an
audience for every subsequent comm. Where the practitioner is unsure, it goes in
`open_questions` and surfaces as a `[GAP]` — not into this block on an assumption.
