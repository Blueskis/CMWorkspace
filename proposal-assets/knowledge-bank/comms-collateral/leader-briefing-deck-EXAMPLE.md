> **This is a format exemplar with invented content. Delete before real use.**

## When this structure works

A change with high people-impact where line managers have to carry the message to their teams.
The deck is not for the team — it is for the manager standing in front of the team, and it has
to survive being presented by someone who read it ten minutes earlier.

## The sequence that worked

1. **Title** — the change, the date, who this pack is for.
2. **Why this, why now** — one slide. Managers get asked this first and cannot say "the
   programme decided."
3. **What's changing** — in the team's terms, not the programme's.
4. **What's not changing** — consistently the slide managers reported as most useful, because
   it is what their teams actually asked about.
5. **Who's affected** — by segment, so a manager can tell which of their people are in scope.
6. **Timeline** — real dates, with anything indicative marked as indicative on the slide.
7. **What we're asking of you** — the manager's own action, separate from their team's.
8. **Support and help** — what to do when someone asks something they can't answer.
9. **Anticipated objections** — the slide that makes the difference.

## The objections slide

Three to five questions the manager will be asked, each with an honest answer, including the
ones without a good answer. A manager ambushed by the first question loses the room for the
rest of the session, and they only get one attempt.

Write the answer to "does this mean job losses?" even when the answer is no — especially when
the answer is no. Ducking it in the pack means it gets improvised in the room.

## Speaker notes

Every content slide had notes. The decks that shipped without them came back with managers
having invented their own framing, and the invented framing is what their teams remembered.
Treat missing notes as a defect, not a nice-to-have.
