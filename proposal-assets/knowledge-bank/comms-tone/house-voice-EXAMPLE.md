> with the firm's own agreed voice rather than editing this one.**

## Register

Write as one person to another person. Second person throughout — "you'll be paid on the
15th", not "employees will be paid on the 15th". The third person is how an organisation
sounds when it is avoiding responsibility for something.

Contractions are fine and make the register warmer. Formality does not equal credibility;
in change comms it usually signals distance at exactly the moment distance is expensive.

## Sentences

Short. One idea per sentence. Where a sentence has an "and" joining two things the reader has
to do, it is two sentences.

Target a reading age of about 12 for anything going to a whole employee base. This is not
condescension — people read work email while doing something else, and complex sentences are
where they stop.

## Openings

Say why this email exists today, in the first line. The reader's first question is "why am I
being told this now", and a comm that opens with programme background answers a question
nobody asked.

Never open with "As you may be aware". Either they are aware, in which case say something
new, or they are not, in which case the phrase makes them feel they missed something.

## The passive voice

Fine when the actor genuinely does not matter ("payslips are issued on the last working day").
A problem when it hides a decision-maker: "it has been decided that" is a sentence with
somebody removed from it, and readers notice.

## Things that erode trust

- Manufactured urgency where there is none. Save it for when it is real.
- "Exciting" applied to a change the reader did not ask for.
- Promising detail "shortly" without a date.
- Describing a reduction in service as an improvement. Say what it is; explain why.
